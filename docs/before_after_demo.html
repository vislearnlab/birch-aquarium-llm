Birch Ask · RAG pipeline audit

What the pipeline changes before a child hears it

Three real questions asked by real kids during pilot testing at Birch Aquarium, each run twice against the same local model (llama3.2:3b): once as a fully generic completion with no retrieval and no system prompt, and once through the actual ask() pipeline this study uses.

Retrieval

Pulls the top 4 chunks from a Wikipedia + Birch Aquarium index, so answers are grounded in a real source instead of parametric memory.

Kid-safety prompt

Forces 2–4 sentences, plain language, no trailing questions, and no mention of any specific aquarium.

Output gate

Screens the answer, strips institution mentions, and flags any number that isn’t actually in the retrieved text.

Gentoo penguin•age 9•pilot participant museumAI06

How fast can they slide on their bellies? Do they slide?

Before — generic, no grounding

You’re referring to turtles! Turtles are known for their ability to move slowly on land, but when it comes to sliding on their bellies, it’s a bit more complicated. Some species of turtles, like the Spurred Tortoise or the Sulcata Tortoise, can indeed slide on their bellies when they feel threatened, scared, or trying to escape. This is often referred to as “tortoise sliding” or “turtle sliding.” When a turtle slides on its belly, it’s usually because it’s trying to: 1. Escape from predators… 2. Regain traction… 3. Move quickly over rough terrain…

Wrong animal, from the first sentence. Nothing in the bare question mentions a penguin, so the model free-associates “sliding on bellies” onto tortoises and invents a whole taxonomy of turtle-sliding behavior that isn’t real.

After — birch-ask pipeline

Gentoo penguins are very good at sliding on their bellies across the snow to get around. They use their feet and flippers to push off the ground, kind of like a sled, and then they slide down hills or over snowdrifts. It’s really fast! They can go up to 25 miles per hour when sliding on their bellies. Zorpie’s still learning about that one! That would be a great question for an expert at Birch Aquarium — ask if you see one today, or on your next visit.

Correct animal and behavior — plus the grounding check catching itself. “25 mph” isn’t actually in the retrieved source, so the output gate’s numeric-grounding check flagged it as unverified and appended the italicized redirect-to-staff line live, rather than letting an ungrounded number stand as fact.

Loggerhead sea turtle•age 9•pilot participant museum010

why is it called loggerhead?

Before — generic, no grounding

The term “loggerhead” refers to the green sea turtle (Chelonia mydas), which has a distinctive head shape that resembles a log or a wedge. In the 17th century, sailors and fishermen used the term “loggerhead” to describe the shape of the sea turtle’s head, which they found similar to the shape of a log or a wedge. Over time, the name “loggerhead” became commonly used to refer to this species of turtle.

Wrong species and invented history. It misattributes the name to the green sea turtle (a different species entirely) and fabricates a 17th-century sailor origin story with specific, false period detail.

After — birch-ask pipeline

The loggerhead sea turtle gets its name because of its very large head compared to its body. It’s like having a big head on a small body! The name “loggerhead” comes from the fact that the turtle has a distinctive head shape, which makes it look a bit different from other turtles.

Correct species, correct etymology. Right animal, and the real reason for the name — a big head — without inventing a backstory to fill the gap.

African penguin•age 7•pilot participant museumAI09

Can their babies stay alive under the snow?

Before — generic, no grounding

You’re referring to polar bears! Polar bears (Ursus maritimus) are well adapted to living in the Arctic environment… When polar bears dig dens in the snow and ice, they can reduce their body temperature to just above freezing (32°F or 0°C) and slow down their heart rate. This allows them to survive for months without eating or drinking. Newborn polar bear cubs rely on their mother’s milk for nutrition and warmth…

Answers about the wrong animal entirely. With no context tying the question to an African penguin, the model defaults to polar bears — a fluent, confident, specific-sounding answer to a question nobody asked.

After — birch-ask pipeline

African penguin babies can’t survive under the snow because they live in warm places near the ocean, not in cold snowy areas. They need to be able to swim and find food in the water to grow strong. The snow would make it hard for them to breathe and get the food they need.

Correctly on-topic, and honest. Directly addresses the premise (African penguins never see snow) instead of drifting to a different species entirely.

grounded in → en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_penguin
